Learn by playing

Short challenges that make you look twice.

Practise at your own pace with Spectrum Explorer, Tone Specialist and Colour in Motion. Choose Classic, Progressive or a fixed difficulty, adjust session length and continue saved progress. Training answers are untimed and offer “I cannot tell”.

Spectrum Explorer

Find the family

Explore neighbouring colour families, notice where one seems to become another, and reveal descriptive vocabulary after each choice.

Tone Specialist

Spot the difference

Pick the odd tile while the game changes one controlled quality at a time: hue, lightness, or chroma.

Colour in Motion

Follow colour in motion

Find colours in layered, animated scenes with shapes and ducks that fly, swim and walk. Decorative artwork remains separate from the exact challenge colours.

Fun while training

Two ways to play with colour.

Match a pair, catch the right colour, and come back for another round. Both games are free, with four difficulties from Easy to Extreme.

Flip & Match

Find every matching pair.

Turn over cards and remember where the colours are. Choose from six board sizes: 6, 12, 16, 20, 30 or 42 cards. Varied colour families keep you looking closely as difficulty increases.

Continue an unfinished game, then copy its seed from the completion summary.

Colour Slice · New in 1.1.1

Slice the target colours.

Follow one to three named target colours and slice matching fruit as it spins and arcs across the screen. Palettes change during play, with a splash and a moment to see the new targets.

Pick a 1-, 2- or 5-minute game. Higher difficulties bring closer colour choices and faster fruit. Missed targets name the colour you missed, without a penalty.

With VoiceOver or Reduce Motion, Slice uses stationary fruit and untimed play.

Daily Colour Mix

A fresh challenge. Your kind of mix.

Combine Spectrum, Tone and Motion exercises in a daily challenge of 10–100 training rounds. Add Flip & Match, a short one-minute Colour Slice game, or both.

Shuffle the activities, or use each game’s separate Keep at the end switch. Resume an unfinished mix and revisit recent runs.

Every completion summary exposes its seed with a copy button. Share a Daily challenge code so a friend can try the same mix with newly arranged questions and objects.

Why practise colour?

Colour is something the visual system can learn about.

The research supports a modest idea: repeated, specific practice can change performance on colour tasks, and learning names can shape how adults categorise what they see. It does not show that a phone game can clinically improve eyesight.

01

Practise fine differences

A 2024 study found lower colour-discrimination thresholds after five days of repeated practice. The effects were strongest around trained colours, and the authors also observed shifts in colour appearance and category boundaries.

Read the Scientific Reports study
02

Learn useful distinctions

Adults trained on newly named green and blue subcategories learned the labels quickly; behavioural and brain-imaging results showed that category learning can influence colour processing.

Read the PNAS study
03

Build vocabulary and attention

Putting words to lightness, chroma, hue, and neighbouring families gives you a repeatable way to compare colours—useful for conversation, art, design, shopping, nature, and simple curiosity.

This is a practical inference from discrimination and category-learning research, not a promised outcome.
What “training” means here

It means practising a task and learning language for it. Improvements may be specific to the trained colours, task, display, or viewing context. ColourTrainer reports performance only inside the trainer.

Including colour-vision deficiency

Practice can support strategies; it cannot replace cone function.

People with colour-vision deficiency may still enjoy the vocabulary, comparison, and self-paced practice. Research reports that dichromatic observers can often name colours accurately by learning subtle visual cues and shared language, even when particular hues remain difficult to discriminate.

That can make training useful as practice in noticing available cues and learning labels. It is not a cure, treatment, screening tool, or promise of normal colour vision.

Learned cues and naming A Visual Neuroscience study describes accurate naming supported by subtle cues and a learned language system. Clinical boundary The US National Eye Institute explains that inherited colour-vision deficiency has no cure, though most people adjust to it.
Accessible by design Training exercises offer “I cannot tell”. Slice supports stationary, untimed play with VoiceOver or Reduce Motion. Everyday display settings remain supported.

Sources, standards, and naming

Reference data is useful—not universal truth.

ColourTrainer uses published technical references to generate reproducible on-screen exercises. Those references do not make a phone a calibrated instrument or settle what every person should call a colour.

Data referenceW3C CSS Color Module Level 4

The app uses a 23 July 2026 snapshot of the CSS named-colour table for stable English identifiers and exact sRGB samples. W3C does not define ColourTrainer’s family boundaries, curriculum, descriptions, or cultural meanings.

Colour mathsOKLab, OKLCH, sRGB, and CIEDE2000

Challenges are authored in OKLCH, converted to tested sRGB output, and may use CIEDE2000 to describe a displayed pair. Numeric distance guides generation; it does not guarantee identical perception.

Important notes

Colour depends on more than the pixels.

Not a medical or diagnostic tool Do not use ColourTrainer to diagnose, screen, treat, or measure colour-vision deficiency. Ask an optometrist or other qualified professional about vision concerns.
Screens are not reality A colour emitted by a screen is not the same physical stimulus as paint, fabric, food, skin, plants, or another real object under changing light.
Displays and rooms differ Panel hardware, gamut, brightness, viewing angle, screen protectors, ambient light, glare, Night Shift, True Tone, and accessibility settings can change appearance.
Names and boundaries differ W3C names are technical English references. People, languages, and cultures may group and name the same continuum differently—and no hard boundary is perfect for everyone.
People and moments differ Colour perception varies between observers and can also be affected by adaptation, fatigue, comfort, and viewing context.
Preview builds may change TestFlight builds can include unfinished exercises, wording, data, artwork, or availability changes before they reach the App Store release.
